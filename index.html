01 · THE SCIENCE

Why roofs overheat

Four things happen between the sun and your ceiling — understanding them is what makes a roof 'cool'.

Sunlight has three parts

Visible light, ultraviolet and infrared. A dark roof absorbs most of all three — and can reach 65–80°C on a hot day.

That heat conducts through the roof

It soaks into the attic and the rooms below — the air conditioner works harder, and the top floor suffers first.

Solar reflectance (albedo)

A cool roof bounces back 60–90% of incoming sunlight; dark materials reflect just 5–25%.

Thermal emittance

Absorbed heat should radiate back to the sky instead of being held. Bare metal reflects but emits poorly — it needs a coating.

same sun · two very different roofs

REFLECT + INSULATE

Two separate jobs, better together: reflection stops heat entering the roof surface at all, while insulation — mineral wool, foam and similar — slows whatever still gets through, and keeps warmth in during cold weather.

04 · HOW IT'S DONE IN PRACTICE

From our model to your roof

The same logic that cooled House B by 10.8°C scales up to real buildings in four steps.

1

PREPARE

Clean dirt; fix cracks and leaks so the coating bonds well.

2

APPLY

Roll or spray the reflective layer — often two coats for even coverage.

3

CURE

Let it cure for a few dry days before full sun.

4

MAINTAIN

Dirt and algae cut reflectivity — wash occasionally.

05 · BENEFITS & TRADE-OFFS

Why it's worth it — and what to watch for

Why it's worth it

  • Cooler indoors — biggest gain on the top floor
  • Lower cooling bills, less strain on air conditioners
  • Longer roof life — fewer thermal swings, less UV damage
  • Smaller urban heat-island effect across cities

What to watch for

  • Cold climates: less winter sun absorbed — heating rises slightly
  • Dirt dulls the surface and weakens the effect over time
  • Glare can bother neighbours in dense settings
